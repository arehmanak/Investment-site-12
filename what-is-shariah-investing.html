What is Shariah-Based Investing?

A plain-language introduction from a capital-markets perspective — the principles it rests on, how they decide what you can own, and the instruments that make it work.

The Idea

A values-based lens on the financial markets

Shariah-based investing — also called Islamic investing — is the practice of putting money to work in the capital markets within the boundaries of Shariah, the moral and legal framework of Islam. It belongs to the wider family of ethical, values-driven investing, but its rules come from a defined body of religious principle rather than personal taste or a single ratings agency.

In practice it asks two questions of an investment that conventional finance does not. First, does the underlying activity create value in a permissible way? Second, is the investment built around real assets and shared risk rather than a guaranteed stream of interest? Almost everything else — the screens, the instruments, the certifications — flows from those two questions.

Because the framework rewards ownership of real, productive businesses and discourages pure money-lending, it naturally tilts investors toward equity and asset-backed instruments and away from conventional interest-bearing debt.

☪️

In one line: Shariah investing channels capital into real economic activity, shares risk and reward fairly, and avoids interest, excessive uncertainty, gambling, and harmful industries.

Foundations

The principles it rests on

A handful of ideas do most of the work — a mix of prohibitions and positive duties that together define what a compliant investment looks like.

🚫

RibaInterest / usury

Earning or paying interest is ruled out. Money is a medium of exchange, not a commodity that grows by itself — so returns must come from genuine enterprise or asset ownership, not from lending at a fixed rate.

🌫️

GhararExcessive uncertainty

Contracts built on excessive ambiguity or hidden information are discouraged. Terms, ownership and outcomes should be clear, which is why highly opaque or purely speculative structures are avoided.

🎲

MaysirGambling

Wealth should not be manufactured from chance alone. Gambling-like bets — where one party's gain is simply another's loss, with no productive activity behind it — sit outside the framework.

✅

Halal activityPermissible business

The underlying business must itself be permissible. Companies earning mainly from alcohol, tobacco, pork, conventional finance, gambling, adult content or weapons are excluded on principle.

🤝

Risk-sharingMusharakah / Mudarabah

Finance should be tied to real assets and shared risk. Profit-and-loss sharing — rather than a return guaranteed regardless of performance — is the ideal, aligning the investor with the enterprise.

🌍

Social benefitMaslaha

Beyond the rules, investing should serve broader well-being and avoid harm. This duty is what connects Shariah investing to the wider ethical- and responsible-investing movement.

How It Works

How the principles become a portfolio

Turning these principles into an actual list of investable securities happens through screening — usually in two layers — followed by an ongoing clean-up step.

01

The business screen (qualitative)

First, what the company does. If its core activity is impermissible — conventional banking and insurance, alcohol, tobacco, gambling, weapons, pork, adult entertainment — it is excluded outright, however profitable it may be.

02

The financial screen (quantitative)

Then, how the company is financed. Even a permissible business can fail the test if it leans too heavily on interest. Financial screens cap things like interest-bearing debt and interest income relative to the company's size. The exact thresholds vary between standards and index providers.

Then: purification. A final step deals with the small, unavoidable slivers of non-compliant income — such as interest a company earns on its idle cash. Investors estimate that fraction of their returns and give an equivalent amount to charity, keeping the remainder clean.

The Instruments

What you can actually own

Islamic capital markets have grown their own toolkit — familiar market instruments re-engineered to fit the principles above.

📈

Screened equities

Ownership stakes in companies that pass both screens. Equity is the natural fit for Shariah investing, because a shareholder genuinely shares in the firm's profit and loss.

🧺

Islamic funds & ETFs

Professionally managed baskets of screened securities, overseen by a Shariah board that handles screening, monitoring and purification on the investor's behalf.

📜

Sukuk

Often called "Islamic bonds," but structurally different. Instead of lending at interest, a sukuk holder owns a share of a real asset or project and earns a portion of the income it produces.

🏢

Islamic REITs

Real-estate trusts holding permissible, income-producing property — with rules on tenants' activities and on how any conventional financing within the trust is handled.

📊

Islamic indices

Benchmarks such as the Dow Jones Islamic Market, S&P Shariah, MSCI Islamic and FTSE Shariah families track pre-screened universes, giving passive investors a ready-made compliant market to follow.

💵

Cash & money-market

For short-term needs, structures based on murabaha (cost-plus sale) or commodity trades stand in for conventional interest-bearing deposits and money-market funds.

Governance

Who decides what counts as compliant?

Compliance isn't self-declared — it rests on scholars, published standards, and the data providers who apply them at scale.

👥

Shariah scholars & boards

Qualified scholars, usually sitting as a Shariah supervisory board, review a fund or product, issue a ruling (fatwa) on its compliance, and keep monitoring it over time.

📐

Standard-setters

Bodies such as AAOIFI and the IFSB publish the accounting, screening and governance standards that bring consistency across markets and countries.

🗂️

Index & data providers

The firms behind the Islamic indices — and the screening apps investors use — apply these standards at scale, publishing the compliant universes that funds and individuals rely on.

Why the source matters: because standards and scholars can differ on the fine print, two providers may occasionally disagree on a borderline stock. Knowing whose methodology sits behind a compliance rating is part of investing thoughtfully.

In Context

Shariah, conventional and ESG investing

Shariah investing overlaps with mainstream ethical investing, but it isn't the same thing.

Conventional No values screen

Optimises purely for risk and return. No ethical filter is applied, and interest-based instruments sit right at the centre of the toolkit.

ESG / SRI Manager-defined

Adds environmental, social and governance preferences — but the criteria are set by asset managers or ratings firms and vary widely. Interest-based instruments are still allowed.

Shariah Rules-based

A defined, faith-based rulebook. It shares ESG's ethical spirit but adds firm prohibitions — most distinctively on interest — and a preference for real-asset, risk-sharing structures.

In short: Shariah investing is a specific, rules-based branch of the broader ethical-investing tree.
