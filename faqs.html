Frequently Asked Questions

Thirty common questions from new investors on Shariah compliance, halal investing in India, and the Indian stock market. Tap any question to reveal the answer.

Shariah / Halal Investing — Basics
Q

What is Shariah-compliant (halal) investing?

It means investing only in ways permitted under Islamic law: avoiding interest (riba), excessive uncertainty (gharar) and gambling (maisir), and steering clear of businesses in prohibited sectors. Returns are expected to come from real, productive economic activity and shared risk rather than from money itself.

Q

Is investing in the stock market halal?

It can be. A share is part-ownership of a real business, which is permissible — provided the company's core activity is permissible and it passes the financial screens. The market itself is just a venue; each company must be assessed individually.

Q

What is riba, and why is it prohibited?

Riba is interest — a guaranteed increase on money lent, earned without sharing real risk or adding value. Islam regards it as unjust, which rules out interest-bearing bonds, conventional fixed deposits and ordinary savings-account interest.

Q

What do gharar and maisir mean?

Gharar is excessive uncertainty or ambiguity in a contract; maisir is gambling or pure speculation. Both are avoided, which is why complex derivatives, betting products and conventional insurance structures are generally excluded.

Q

Which business sectors are screened out?

Commonly excluded: conventional banking and lending, insurance, alcohol, tobacco, pork, gambling and casinos, adult entertainment, and weapons — and sometimes cinema and music. The exact list depends on the screening standard used.

Q

What financial ratios are used to screen a stock?

Beyond the business test, scholars apply financial limits. Typical AAOIFI-style thresholds are: interest-bearing debt below about 30–33% of market cap (some standards use 25% of assets), cash plus interest-bearing securities below roughly a third, and impermissible income below 5% of total revenue.

Q

What is "purification" of income?

If a company earns a small amount of incidental impermissible income (for example, interest on its cash), the matching slice of your return is considered impure. Purification means giving that portion away to charity without expecting reward. Screening apps and some funds estimate the per-share amount.

Q

Do I have to be Muslim to invest this way?

No. Anyone can use Shariah-compliant products. Many non-Muslims choose them as a form of ethical or socially responsible investing, since the screens naturally avoid debt-heavy companies and harmful sectors.

Shariah-Based Investing in India
Q

What halal investment options exist in India?

Shariah-compliant equity mutual funds, screened direct stocks, a Shariah index-fund route, smallcases, gold, real estate, and — mostly via global platforms — sukuk. Each option needs its own compliance check before you commit.

Q

Which Shariah-compliant mutual funds are available in India?

The main ones include the Tata Ethical Fund (active, since 1996), Taurus Ethical Fund (active, since 2009), Quantum Ethical Fund (since 2024) and the UTI Nifty500 Shariah Index Fund (passive). Availability and details change, so verify current status before investing.

Q

What's the difference between an active ethical fund and a Shariah index fund?

An active ethical fund has a manager picking screened stocks aiming to beat a benchmark — higher cost and some manager risk. A Shariah index fund passively tracks a screened index such as the Nifty 500 Shariah, with lower cost and no stock-selection risk.

Q

Are there Shariah-compliant ETFs in India?

India has had Shariah index and ETF-style options over the years, and newer passive index funds track Shariah indices. Always confirm a specific product is currently live and check its liquidity and tracking before relying on it.

Q

What are smallcases, and are there halal ones?

A smallcase is a ready-made basket of stocks built around a theme. Some are screened for Shariah compliance — avoiding prohibited sectors and high-debt firms. Check who screens the basket, against what standard, and how often it is reviewed.

Q

Can I invest in gold the halal way in India?

Yes. Physical gold and gold held in screened, asset-backed forms are generally permissible, and some apps offer digital gold backed by physical metal. Avoid interest-based or purely speculative gold products, and follow the rules on taking possession.

Q

Is real estate — or are REITs — halal in India?

Direct ownership of property for rent or appreciation is generally permissible if it isn't financed with interest. REITs and some property platforms may qualify if they avoid interest-heavy structures and impermissible tenants — these need a case-by-case review.

Q

Are sukuk (Islamic bonds) available in India?

Sukuk are asset-backed certificates that pay returns from real assets rather than interest. They aren't widely issued domestically in India yet, so Indian investors usually access them through global funds or platforms — often USD-denominated, which adds currency considerations.

Q

How do I verify that a stock is Shariah-compliant?

Use a screening tool such as Islamicly, Zoya or Musaffa. They check business activity and financial ratios and give a compliant or non-compliant status that's updated regularly. Borderline cases are worth a closer manual look at the company's filings.

Indian Stock Market — Basics for New Investors
Q

What do NSE, BSE, Sensex and Nifty mean?

The NSE and BSE are India's two main stock exchanges. The Sensex (30 BSE companies) and the Nifty 50 (50 NSE companies) are benchmark indices that track the overall direction of the market.

Q

What is a demat and trading account, and do I need one?

A demat account holds your shares electronically, and a trading account is used to place buy and sell orders — they're usually opened together with a broker. For mutual funds you can invest directly through the fund house or a platform without needing a demat.

Q

What is SEBI, and how does it protect investors?

SEBI (the Securities and Exchange Board of India) is the market regulator. It sets rules for exchanges, brokers and mutual funds, enforces disclosure, and runs grievance mechanisms — all aimed at keeping the market fair and protecting investors.

Q

What is a SIP, and how does it work?

A Systematic Investment Plan invests a fixed amount at regular intervals — usually monthly — into a fund. It spreads your entry across market highs and lows (rupee-cost averaging) and builds discipline. Many funds allow SIPs from as little as ₹100–₹500.

Q

What's the difference between large-, mid- and small-cap?

Large-cap means the biggest, most established companies, which tend to be steadier. Mid- and small-caps are smaller firms with higher growth potential but more volatility. Many investors hold a mix to balance stability and growth.

Q

How are equity investments taxed in India?

As a general guide only: gains on listed equity or equity funds held beyond a year are treated as long-term and taxed at a lower rate, while gains within a year are short-term and taxed higher. Exact rates, exemptions and NRI treatment change over time — confirm current rules with a tax professional.

Q

How much money do I need to start?

Very little. Many SIPs begin at ₹100–₹500 per month, and fractional or curated baskets lower the entry further. Starting small and staying consistent matters far more than the initial amount.

For Gulf-Based NRI Investors
Q

Can Gulf-based NRIs invest in Indian mutual funds?

Yes. NRIs in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar, Oman and Bahrain can invest in Indian mutual funds, typically after opening an NRE or NRO account and completing KYC. Routes include fund houses, discount brokers, full-service brokers and distributors.

Q

What is the difference between an NRE and NRO account?

An NRE account holds foreign earnings converted into rupees and is fully repatriable. An NRO account is mainly for income earned within India and has repatriation limits. Either can fund investments, with different tax and repatriation treatment.

Q

Why do USA/Canada NRIs face restrictions but Gulf NRIs usually don't?

NRIs in the USA and Canada trigger FATCA reporting obligations that many Indian fund houses choose not to support, so they often restrict those investors. Gulf-based NRIs generally don't face this barrier.

Q

What is GIFT City, and why does it matter for NRIs?

GIFT City is India's International Financial Services Centre. It lets global and NRI investors access India-linked products through a USD-denominated, often tax-efficient framework with simplified compliance — an alternative to the direct domestic route.

Getting Started & Risk
Q

How do I actually start, step by step?

A simple path: (1) open an NRE/NRO account if you're an NRI; (2) complete mutual-fund KYC; (3) pick a route — fund house, discount broker, full-service broker or distributor; (4) choose one or two screened or ethical funds; (5) start a small monthly SIP; and (6) stay invested for the long term, reviewing once or twice a year.

Q

Active fund, index fund, or direct stocks — where should a beginner start?

Many beginners start with one or two screened mutual funds or a Shariah index fund, where a manager or the index handles compliance and diversification. Direct stocks and curated baskets can be added later, once you're comfortable using a screening tool and reading the basics of a company.

Q

How often should I check or rebalance my portfolio?

For long-term investors, once or twice a year is usually enough — to confirm holdings are still compliant and your mix still fits your goals. Reacting to every short-term market move tends to hurt more than it helps.

Q

What happens if a stock I hold stops being compliant?

Compliance isn't permanent — a company's debt or income mix can change. If a holding fails the screen, the usual guidance is to exit it within a reasonable window and purify any impermissible portion of the gains. In a fund, the manager or index handles this for you.

Q

What are the main risks I should understand?

The key ones: market volatility (equities can fall 20–30% in the short term), currency risk (returns are in INR while you may spend in AED or USD), concentration and liquidity risk in smaller funds or stocks, and compliance risk if a holding loses Shariah status. A long horizon and good diversification help manage all of them.

Q

How do I avoid "halal" investment scams?

Be very wary of schemes promising fixed or unusually high "guaranteed" returns in the name of being halal — genuine investing shares real risk. Prefer SEBI-registered products and platforms, avoid anything you don't fully understand, and when in doubt consult a SEBI-registered adviser or a qualified scholar.
